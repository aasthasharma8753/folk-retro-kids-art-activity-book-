India is a country in the continent of Asia. It sits on its own peninsula, with the Arabian Sea to the west, the Bay of Bengal to the east, and the Indian Ocean to the south.

To the north, the Himalayas — the tallest mountains on Earth — separate India from its neighbours. India shares borders with several countries, including Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar.

Did You Know?

The Thar Desert in the west and the rainy Sundarbans mangrove forest in the east are both part of India — two totally different worlds in one country!

Subcontinent

A very large landmass that is part of a continent but separated by mountains or sea. India is part of the South Asian subcontinent.

Chapter 04

Many Kitchens, Many Flavours

There is no single "Indian food." Different regions cook very differently — rice and coconut are common in the south, wheat breads and dairy in the north, fish and mustard oil near the coasts, and every family has its own favourite way of making a dish.

Simple Vegetable Poha

A quick flattened-rice breakfast from Maharashtra, in western India

Serves 415 minutesStovetop
Ingredients
  • 2 cups flattened rice (poha)
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 2 tbsp peanuts
  • ½ tsp mustard seeds
  • ¼ tsp turmeric
  • A few curry leaves (optional)
  • Squeeze of lemon & salt
Steps
  1. Rinse the flattened rice gently in a bowl of water, then drain. Let it soften.
  2. A grown-up heats a little oil, adds mustard seeds and peanuts until they pop and toast.
  3. Add chopped onion and curry leaves; cook until soft.
  4. Stir in turmeric, then add the softened rice and salt. Mix gently.
  5. Cook for two minutes, then squeeze fresh lemon on top before serving.
Ask a grown-up to help with the stove

Chapter 09

Around the Festival Wheel

Not every festival is celebrated by every family — celebrations depend on religion, region and community. Here are ten you might come across during the year.

Diwali

Widely celebrated festival of lights among Hindus, Jains and Sikhs. Oil lamps, rangoli, sweets and fireworks.

Holi

Spring festival celebrated mainly in northern India with coloured powder, water, music and festive foods.

Eid

Celebrated by Muslim communities marking the end of Ramadan or a time of sacrifice, with prayers, new clothes and feasting.

Vaisakhi

Harvest and new-year festival important to Sikh and Punjabi communities, with music, dance and gurdwara visits.

Onam

Harvest festival celebrated in Kerala with flower rangoli (pookalam), boat races and a grand feast.

Pongal

Tamil harvest festival giving thanks with a sweet rice dish cooked until it overflows the pot.

Durga Puja

Major festival in West Bengal and beyond honouring the goddess Durga with elaborate decorated pandals.

Navratri

Nine nights of dance (garba/dandiya) and devotion celebrated in many regions, especially Gujarat.

Ganesh Chaturthi

Festival honouring Ganesha, especially popular in Maharashtra, with music, processions and clay idols.

Christmas

Celebrated by Christian communities across India, especially in states like Goa and Kerala, with carols and festive food.

Remember

No single family celebrates all of these — that's what makes India's calendar so rich and varied!

Chapter 10

Many Kinds of Stories

India's storytelling traditions include several different kinds of stories, and it helps to know the difference: religious traditions are sacred beliefs held by a faith community; mythology includes traditional sacred narratives passed down over centuries; folklore is stories shared within a community or region; fables are short tales that teach a lesson, often using animal characters; and history is what we know actually happened.

Respected Figures

Figures such as Krishna, Rama, Hanuman, Ganesha and Durga appear in Hindu religious tradition and mythology and are meaningful to many families. We introduce them here with respect, not as cartoon characters.

Panchatantra

A classic collection of animal fables, originally written to teach wisdom and clever thinking.

Jataka Tales

Stories connected to Buddhist tradition, often describing the Buddha's past lives through animal fables.

Chapter 12 · History

When India Was Ruled by Britain

Starting in the 1600s, a British trading company called the East India Company began operating in India and slowly gained more control over land and trade. Over time, Britain came to rule large parts of India directly, and Indian people had little say in the decisions that shaped their own country.

Colonial rule reorganised land ownership, trade, taxation and industry to benefit Britain, which caused serious hardship for many Indian communities — including famines made worse by policy decisions, and damage to local industries like handloom weaving. This was not simply an exchange between cultures; it was a system of unequal power.

People across India resisted colonial rule in many ways — through protest, political organising, boycotts, writing, and mass movements — for well over half a century. India became an independent country in 1947.

Chapter 12 · History

The Road to Independence

1600s–1850s

The East India Company expands its trade posts into growing political and territorial control across India.

1857

A major uprising against East India Company rule spreads across northern and central India. Afterward, the British Crown takes over direct rule.

Early 1900s

Organised independence movements grow, with newspapers, political parties and public gatherings calling for self-rule.

1920s–1940s

Large-scale non-cooperation and civil-disobedience movements — including strikes, boycotts and marches — spread across the country, involving millions of people.

1947

India becomes an independent nation.
